Transition guide

AiStandardSurface → PxrSurface

Same job, different toolbox. This guide maps Arnold's AiStandardSurface controls onto RenderMan's PxrSurface, explains what each parameter is actually doing to the light, and flags the handful of places the two shaders think about materials differently.

The mental model

Both shaders are "one node does everything" surface shaders — you're not switching to a node-graph-per-material workflow. PxrSurface is built the same way AiStandardSurface is: a stack of light-response layers (diffuse, specular, a coat, subsurface, and so on) that all live inside one shader and blend together. The stack is just organized and named a little differently.

Same seven jobs, different names for most of them. The one layer that genuinely works differently — not just a rename — is Specular, because that's where "metalness" lives in Arnold and doesn't exist as a single slider in PxrSurface. More on that below.

Layer 1

Diffuse / Base

This is the matte, non-shiny part of the material — the color you'd see under totally flat, shadowless lighting. In Arnold it's called Base; in PxrSurface it's called Diffuse. Same job, new label.

Diffuse Color / Gain

Base Color → Diffuse Color, and Base Weight → Diffuse Gain. These map one-to-one — texture your Diffuse Color exactly the way you'd texture Base Color.

Diffuse Roughness

Both shaders have a Diffuse Roughness control that exists for one reason: perfectly smooth diffuse math (called "Lambertian") makes every matte surface look faintly like painted plastic. Raising Diffuse Roughness breaks that up.

Think of it as

Chalk versus a freshly painted matte wall. Both are "not shiny," but chalk scatters light more unevenly because its surface is powdery at a microscopic level. Diffuse Roughness dials a material from "painted wall" (0) toward "chalk or dry clay" (higher values).

↔ Coming from Arnold

This one behaves the same way in both shaders — if you had a value you liked in AiStandardSurface, it's a safe starting point in PxrSurface too.

Layer 2 — the big one

Specular & "metalness"

This is where the two shaders genuinely diverge, so it's worth slowing down here. Specular is the shiny, mirror-like reflection on top of the diffuse color — think of the diffuse layer as the "paint" and specular as the "varnish" sitting on top of it.

Specular Roughness

Works the same way in both shaders: it controls how blurry the reflection is.

0.0 — mirror-sharp reflection 1.0 — soft, diffuse-like reflection

Think of it as

A mirror versus a smudged mirror. At 0, you get a clean reflection of your environment. As roughness rises, that reflection smears out — like breathing fog onto glass — until at 1.0 it barely looks reflective at all.

Why there's no "Metalness" slider

In AiStandardSurface, flipping Metalness toward 1 does two things at once: it tints the specular reflection using Base Color, and it fades out the diffuse layer, since real metals don't have a diffuse component — all the light bounces off the surface, none of it scatters underneath.

PxrSurface doesn't bundle that behavior into one slider. Instead, you get direct control over the two colors that make up a Fresnel reflection:

  • Specular Face Color — the reflection color when you're looking straight at the surface.
  • Specular Edge Color — the reflection color at a grazing, glancing angle.

Non-metals (skin, plastic, wood) reflect a dim, colorless highlight head-on and a brighter, colorless highlight at grazing angles — so Face Color stays low and neutral, and Edge Color stays near white. Metals (gold, copper, chrome) reflect a tinted highlight even head-on — so for a metal, you push both Face Color and Edge Color toward the metal's actual color (e.g. warm yellow-gold for gold), and you manually turn Diffuse Gain down toward 0, since a metal shouldn't have a visible diffuse layer underneath.

↔ Coming from Arnold

There is no one-click "make this metal" switch. Recreating a metal look means: tint both Specular Face and Edge Color to the metal's color, and manually zero out (or nearly zero out) Diffuse Gain. Skipping the second step is the most common reason a converted metal material looks "washed out" or plasticky in PxrSurface.

Fresnel Mode: Artistic vs. Physical

PxrSurface gives you a choice for how the specular color blends between Face and Edge:

  • Artistic mode — you paint Face Color and Edge Color directly, with a Fresnel Exponent to control how quickly the surface transitions between them. This is the closest feeling to how you already think in Arnold: pick colors by eye, adjust until it reads right.
  • Physical mode — instead of hand-picking colors, you drive the reflection from real-world Specular IOR (index of refraction) and, for metals, an Extinction Coefficient. This is more physically accurate but less immediately intuitive — you're dialling in numbers from real material data rather than eyeballing a color.

For most lookdev work, Artistic mode is the easier starting point if you're used to picking colors by eye in Arnold.

Layer 3

Clearcoat

Coat in Arnold becomes Clearcoat in PxrSurface. Both represent a thin, glassy top layer sitting over everything else — car paint lacquer, a screen protector, wet varnish on wood.

Think of it as

A layer of glass laid over the material. It adds its own small, sharp highlight on top of whatever's underneath, independent of the base specular layer, which is why car paint reads as "shiny in two different ways at once."

Clearcoat gets its own Face Color, Edge Color, Roughness, and IOR, completely separate from the main Specular layer below it — so you can make the coat sharp and clear while the base metal flake underneath is rougher, exactly like the Coat layer in AiStandardSurface.

↔ Coming from Arnold

Behavior and intent match closely — this is one of the more predictable translations. Default IOR in both shaders lands around 1.5, a reasonable starting point for most clearcoats.

Layer 4

Sheen → Fuzz

Arnold's Sheen becomes Fuzz in PxrSurface. Both exist for the same reason: cloth, dust, and fine fibers catch light at grazing angles in a soft, velvety way that the main specular layer doesn't capture well.

Think of it as

The pale glow around the silhouette of a velvet couch or a dusty countertop when it's backlit — light catching countless tiny fibers or dust particles standing up off the surface, rather than one clean reflective surface.

Fuzz gives you a Weight, Color, and Roughness, the same conceptual controls as Sheen. The main difference is under the hood: Fuzz reuses a hair-shading model, so its roughness response curve doesn't line up 1-to-1 with Sheen's — treat any ported value as a starting point to re-eyeball, not a guaranteed match.

Layer 5

Subsurface scattering

Both shaders simulate light entering a surface, bouncing around inside it, and exiting somewhere nearby — the effect that makes skin, wax, marble, and milk look soft and slightly translucent instead of flat.

Think of it as

Shining a flashlight through your fingers. The light doesn't bounce straight off your skin — it goes in, scatters around inside, and comes back out reddened and softened. That's subsurface scattering.

↔ Coming from Arnold

This is the layer least likely to port over with the same numbers. Arnold's Subsurface uses a Scale + Radius pair that's fairly forgiving to eyeball. PxrSurface's Subsurface parameters are tied more directly to physical scattering distances, so a "Radius" value that looked right in Arnold won't automatically look right here — plan to re-tune this layer by eye against a reference (skin, wax, etc.) rather than porting numbers directly.

Layer 6

Emission → Glow

Arnold's Emission / Emission Color becomes PxrSurface's Glow — a straightforward rename. Both make a surface appear to give off its own light: screens, lava, lit signage, embers.

Worth remembering for both shaders: this makes the surface look bright, but it doesn't efficiently light the rest of the scene the way an actual light source does. Treat it as a texture/look effect, not a lighting tool.

Layer 7 — advanced

Transmission / Glass

Arnold's Transmission and PxrSurface's Glass both handle see-through materials — actual glass, clear liquid, thin plastic. Most character and hard-surface lookdev never touches this layer, so this guide keeps it brief: if you're building glass or liquid, expect a re-tuning pass rather than a direct value port, for the same reason as Subsurface above — the underlying math isn't parameterized identically.

Common pitfalls

⚠ "My metal looks like grey plastic"

You tinted Specular Face/Edge Color but forgot to lower Diffuse Gain. A visible diffuse layer under a metal reflection is almost always the culprit — see the Specular section above.

⚠ "Skin/wax looks too solid, not soft"

Subsurface values ported directly from Arnold's Radius/Scale rarely hold up. Re-tune by eye rather than trusting the numbers.

⚠ "The clearcoat isn't visible"

Check Clearcoat's own Roughness — it defaults to a very sharp, low-roughness look, so on a rough base material it can read as barely-there unless the coat itself is kept nearly smooth.

⚠ "Colors look flatter than in Arnold"

Double-check you're comparing Face Color (head-on) against Face Color, not against Arnold's single Specular Color — PxrSurface's two-color Fresnel model means a like-for-like comparison needs both Face and Edge Color set intentionally, not just one.
